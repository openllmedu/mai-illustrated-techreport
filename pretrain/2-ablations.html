Section 2.2

Ablation Methodology

How every design decision was rigorously tested at multiple scales before adoption

TL;DR

A scaling ladder of 9 model sizes (365M to 35B active params) tests every change at small, medium, and large scale. Efficiency Gain (EG) quantifies improvements: EG=1.3 means the baseline needs 30% more compute to match. Both FLOPs-based and wall-clock-time-based EG are tracked to catch designs that are theoretically good but practically slow.

The Core Problem

A change that helps a small model might not help — or might even hurt — a large one. The team needed a systematic way to avoid investing months of large-scale training on a design choice that only works at small scale.

Scaling Ladder

They built a family of 9 models (365M to 35.6B active parameters) sharing the exact same architecture at different scales. The full configuration is determined by a single parameter: the number of layers (L). Everything else scales deterministically:

  • Hidden size D = L × 256/3
  • Query heads = L (rounded to nearest multiple of 16)
  • Dense FFN = 2× hidden size
  • Expert FFN = 3× compressed size
  • Always 512 experts, top-8 routing, 8 KV heads
Example — L=24: Hidden size = 24 × 256/3 = 2,048. Query heads = 24 (already a multiple of 16). Dense FFN = 2 × 2,048 = 4,096. This gives you a 1.5B-active / 30B-total model. Scale up to L=78 (the final model): hidden = 78 × 256/3 = 6,656, query heads = 80 (nearest multiple of 16). Same blueprint, just bigger numbers.

Say the team wants to test whether LatentMoE compression helps. They first train L12 (365M active params) for ~500B tokens — takes a few GPU-hours. If EG > 1.0, they repeat at L24 (1.5B active) for ~1T tokens — takes a day. Still positive? Test at L36 (4B active) for several days. Only after confirming the benefit scales across 3+ sizes do they commit it to L78, which will train for months on 8,192 GPUs.

Efficiency Gain (EG)

To quantify whether a design change is worth it, the team uses Efficiency Gain:

EG = (compute the baseline needs to match the new design) / (compute the new design actually used)

An EG of 1.3 means the baseline would need 30% more compute to reach the same loss. The metric can be defined in terms of:

  • EGFLOPsFloating-Point Operations — the standard unit for measuring computational cost. One FLOP = one multiply or add on a floating-point number. Training MAI-Base-1 required roughly 10²³ FLOPs total.Learn more — theoretical compute, ignoring implementation efficiency
  • EGTime — actual wall-clock time, accounting for GPU utilization
Worked example: Suppose the baseline model reaches NLL = 2.45 after 1022 FLOPs of training. A new design (say, LatentMoE) reaches that same NLL = 2.45 after only 7.7 × 1021 FLOPs. Then EGFLOPs = 1022 / 7.7 × 1021 = 1.30 — meaning LatentMoE is 30% more compute-efficient. But if LatentMoE's custom kernels are slow and the 7.7 × 1021 FLOPs take 12 days instead of 10, then EGTime = 10/12 = 0.83 — it's actually 17% slower in real time despite being theoretically more efficient.
A design might look great in FLOPs (EGFLOPs = 1.03) but be impractical if it's slower to run on real hardware (EGTime = 0.82). The team tracks both to avoid this trap.

Figure 3 — Efficiency Gain vs. Sparsity

As the number of experts increases (x-axis), EG consistently improves across all categories (code, math, STEM). The architecture scales well with more sparsity. MAI-Base-1 uses 512 experts (top-8) as a balance between quality and practical efficiency.

The ladder + EG methodology ensures no design choice is adopted based on small-scale results alone. Every decision must prove itself at multiple scales before being committed to the final model.
