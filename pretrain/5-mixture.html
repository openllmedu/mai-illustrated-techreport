Section 2.5

Data Mixture Selection

How they chose proportions of code vs. math vs. web — and a surprising discovery

TL;DR

Selecting the right proportions of code, math, STEM, and web data is crucial and surprisingly hard. The team discovered rank non-invariance: a STEM-heavy mix that won at small scale was overtaken by a code-heavy mix at 23B scale, due to hidden duplication in STEM data. The final mixture was found through hierarchical local/global optimization, validated at progressively larger scales.

With hundreds of data sources, the central question is: what proportion of each should the model see? Why It's Hard

  • Scale-dependent effects — a dataset helpful for small models can hurt large ones
  • Cross-dataset interactions — the value of dataset A depends on what else is in the mix
  • Multi-epoch effects — repeating a high-quality dataset too many times causes overfittingWhen a model memorizes its training data rather than learning general patterns. An overfitted model performs well on data it's seen before but poorly on new, unseen data. Excessive repetition of the same content is a common cause.Learn more
  • Vast search space — hundreds of datasets, continuous weight parameters, exhaustive search impossible

Scaling Laws Context

The data mixture problem is deeply connected to scaling laws — the empirical relationships between model size, training data, and compute. Two landmark findings shaped the industry:

  • Kaplan et al. (2020, OpenAI) found loss scales as a power law with model size and data across 7+ orders of magnitude. The conclusion: bigger models are more sample-efficient, so train very large models on "modest" data. This led to GPT-3 (175B params, only 300B tokens).
  • Chinchilla (Hoffmann et al., 2022, DeepMind) overturned this: models were undertrained, not undersized. For compute-optimal training, use ~20 tokens per parameter. Their 70B model trained on 1.4T tokens beat the 280B Gopher trained on 300B tokens.

MAI-Base-1 trains at ~30 tokens per active parameter (30T tokens / ~1T total params), close to Chinchilla-optimal but with the MoE efficiency advantage: 30T tokens train 35B active parameters at ~857:1 ratio — far beyond Chinchilla's 20:1, deliberately over-training to maximize inference-time quality.

Modern models have pushed dramatically past Chinchilla ratios. LLaMA 3 8B trains on 15T tokens (1,875:1 ratio). Gemma 2 9B uses 8T tokens (889:1). The universal trend: train smaller models much longer, making each deployed token cheaper at inference time while investing more in training compute.

A Surprising Discovery: Rank Non-Invariance

A common assumption in data mixing: if mixture A beats mixture B at small scale, A will also beat B at large scale. The team found this is not always true.

They tested stem-heavy-mix (more STEM data) vs. code-heavy-mix (more code). At small scale, stem-heavy-mix won on STEM evaluations as expected. But at 23B scale (20T tokens), the ranking flipped — code-heavy-mix overtook it on STEM.

Figure 6 — Rank Non-Invariance

Left: stem-heavy-mix wins at small compute but loses at large scale. Right: STEM NLL during training — the curves cross midway, with the code-heavy mix eventually winning despite having less STEM data.

The culprit: two STEM datasets with high quality but hidden duplication and low diversity. They helped at small scale but their lack of diversity hurt at large scale (11.8% of stem-heavy-mix vs. only 0.3% of code-heavy-mix).

This finding challenged a core assumption and led the team to put much more emphasis on scaling performance of data mixtures, not just their fixed-scale performance.

Hierarchical Optimization

The final mixture was found through alternating local and global searches:

Local Search

Keep category weights fixed, tune within a category. E.g., vary the weights between code files, PRs, and commits while keeping STEM, web, etc. frozen.

Global Search

Keep within-category proportions fixed, tune between categories. E.g., increase STEM weight while decreasing web weight proportionally.

Max 8 repetitions of any dataset. Candidates validated at progressively larger scales before final selection.

Figure 7 — Dataset Contributions to Physics Performance

For a graduate-level physics evaluation: PDF datasets, math, and STEM web data clearly help. General web is neutral. Code doesn't help physics specifically (but is critical for other capabilities). This kind of analysis guides the local search within categories.

Data mixture optimization is an active area with significant headroom. The rank non-invariance finding — where the best small-scale mix is not the best large-scale mix — fundamentally changed the team's approach to validation.
