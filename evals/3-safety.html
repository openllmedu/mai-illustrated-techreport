Section 4.3

Internal Safety Evaluation

Measuring the safety-helpfulness tradeoff and resistance to adversarial jailbreak attacks.

TL;DR

MAI-Thinking-1 achieves a favorable safety-helpfulness balance, outperforming Sonnet 4.6 on 5 of 8 harm categories. Jailbreak resistance is comparable to Sonnet and Opus across foundational, compositional, and adaptive attack types (~9,500 prompts tested). The model's safety comes from model-level training, not API-level content filters.

The Safety-Helpfulness Tradeoff

A model that refuses every request is perfectly "safe" but useless. A model that answers everything is maximally "helpful" but dangerous. The real challenge is being safe on harmful requests and helpful on benign ones.

The team measures this with an internal benchmark: they give the model low-risk prompts (where it should answer helpfully) and high-risk prompts (where it should refuse). The over-refusal rateThe percentage of legitimate, safe requests that the model incorrectly refuses to answer. Over-refusal is a common failure mode in safety-trained models — they become overly cautious and refuse to answer benign questions about sensitive topics.Learn more measures how often it wrongly refuses the safe ones.

Jailbreak Resistance

The team tests resistance to adversarial attacks with ~9,500 jailbreak prompts in three difficulty buckets:

Foundational Techniques

Single-step transformations: jailbreak wrappers, prompt templates, simple rewording. The easiest attacks to defend against.

Compositional Techniques

Multiple transformations combined: template-based attacks (PyRIT), persuasion-based rewrites (PAP), and mixed-language variants. Harder to catch because the harmful intent is obscured through layers of transformation.

Adaptive Techniques

Interactive, search-based, or multi-turn attacks: Tree of Attacks (TAP), crescendo attacks that gradually escalate over multiple turns. The hardest to defend against because the attacker adapts based on the model's responses.

Evaluation Methodology

The internal safety evaluation uses a two-stage process designed to distinguish genuine safety defects from over-cautious behavior on benign-but-sensitive topics:

Stage 1: User Request Classification

Each request is classified along five dimensions by LLM judges (with majority-vote aggregation to control variance):

DimensionWhat It Captures
Harm categoryWhich policy area the request engages and whether it violates a specific rule
Sensitivity levelOrdinal scale based on scope (individual vs. broad) and severity (worst-case misuse)
User intentBinary: good intent possible vs. malicious intent. Dangerous topics alone are not evidence of malice
User scenarioPragmatic goal: information-seeking, guidance, creative generation, scenario play
Jailbreak detectionScreens for adversarial wrapping and recovers the underlying request for honest evaluation

When a jailbreak wrapper is detected, an extraction step recovers the underlying request, and subsequent classification operates on that recovered intent. This prevents wrapper-specific overfitting from inflating safety scores.

Stage 2: Response Strategy Generation

The Stage 1 classification feeds a rubric judge that emits a per-request response specification covering: policy constraints, appropriate refusal posture (compliance, partial refusal, or full refusal depending on intent and severity), and prescribed tone. For requests classified as harmless, the rubric explicitly penalizes hedging and unwarranted refusal.

Prompt Pool Construction

The evaluation prompt pool comes from three sources: public safety benchmarks, consumer Copilot logs (with PII filtered, opt-out respected), and prompts collected by external vendors against the policy taxonomy. Every prompt is annotated along the 5 classification dimensions.

From this pool, the team selects the most informative prompts using an Item Response TheoryA statistical framework from psychometrics that models how individual items (questions/prompts) discriminate between test-takers of different ability levels. In AI safety evaluation, IRT identifies which prompts are most informative for distinguishing models of different safety levels, allowing more efficient evaluation with fewer prompts.Learn more (IRT)-based automated test assembly approach, upweighting prompts where the model is not always failing or always passing — focusing evaluation budget on the discriminative boundary.

Threshold Setting

Every release candidate must pass acceptance thresholds on a fixed bundle of safety evaluations without regressing against the previous release. The thresholds are derived from a Pareto analysisAn optimization technique that identifies the best achievable tradeoffs between competing objectives. In safety evaluation, it maps the frontier of achievable safety vs. helpfulness vs. quality combinations, placing the threshold at the "elbow" where marginal safety gains begin extracting disproportionate quality costs.Learn more of the safety-helpfulness-quality tradeoff surface: the threshold is placed at a fixed percentile along the frontier of currently achievable recipes, making the bar track what is achievable rather than a stale historical floor.
