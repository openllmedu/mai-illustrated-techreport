Chapter 1

Microsoft Built an AI That Learns to Think from Scratch

MAI-Thinking-1 is a new AI model by Microsoft that can solve complex math, write code, and reason through problems — all without distillation (copying intelligence from other AI models).

TL;DR

Microsoft built a large AI model called MAI-Thinking-1 from the ground up. Instead of using distillation (training by imitating other AI systems), it was pre-trained on human-written text and then taught to reason using reinforcement learning (RL) — a process where the model improves by practicing and getting feedback. The result is a model that scores near-perfectly on hard math competitions and can fix real-world software bugs.

01

What is MAI-Thinking-1?

The "what" in one paragraph

MAI-Thinking-1 is a reasoning modelAn AI model specifically designed to "think through" problems step-by-step, rather than just predicting the next word. It writes out its reasoning before giving an answer. — an AI that doesn't just predict text, but actually works through problems step by step. It was built entirely by Microsoft AI (MAI) without distillation (borrowing intelligence from other AI models).

It's a sparse Mixture of Experts (MoE)Instead of one giant network, the model has 512 small "expert" sub-networks. For any given question, only 8 of these experts activate — making it efficient despite its huge total size. model with about 1 trillion total parameters, but only 35 billion are active for any given question.

What does "35B active out of 1T" mean? The model has 512 expert sub-networks. When processing a token (e.g., the word "velocity" in a physics question), a learned router scores all 512 experts and activates only the top 8. Those 8 experts have ~35B parameters combined. The other 504 experts sit idle for that token — their parameters exist but consume zero compute. This is why the model can be massive (1T total) yet run efficiently.
02

Three Design Principles

The philosophy behind how it was built

1

Learn, Don't Copy

Capabilities should be learned, not inherited. Intelligence imitated through distillation lacks the steerability and robustness essential to long, enduring climbs.

Many AI labs use distillationA technique where a smaller "student" model learns by copying the outputs of a larger "teacher" model. It's faster but the student never truly understands the material — it just mimics answers. (training new models to imitate existing ones) as a shortcut. Microsoft chose the harder path: pre-training the model on real human knowledge so its abilities are genuine and can keep improving.

2

Keep It Simple

Simplicity is sustainable. We favor simple, scalable recipes; clean, trustworthy data; and transparent infrastructure.

Rather than stacking clever tricks, they focused on clean, well-organized data and straightforward training methods that can be scaled up reliably. Simple approaches are easier to debug, reproduce, and improve over time.

3

Test Everything

Scientific rigor avoids shortcuts. Every decision must be testable through data-driven ladders, ablations, and evaluations.

Every architectural choice was validated through systematic experiments called ablationsA scientific method where you remove or change one component at a time to measure its impact. Like testing a recipe by leaving out one ingredient to see what it contributes.. Nothing was assumed — if a design choice couldn't be proven to help, it wasn't kept.

03

How Was It Trained?

Three phases, from reading to reasoning

Training happened in three major phases:

Phase 1

Pre-training: Reading Everything

The model was pre-trained on 30 trillion tokens of human-written content — web pages, books, academic papers, GitHub code, news articles, and more. Crucially, no synthetic data (AI-generated text) was used. This gives the model broad knowledge about the world.

How much is 30 trillion tokens? That's roughly 22.5 trillion words — the equivalent of about 30 million books. The raw crawl was far larger; aggressive deduplication removed 47.5% of web data, and quality filtering cut another large fraction. Only the highest-quality fraction of the internet made it into training.
Phase 2

Mid-training: Specializing

An additional 3.55 trillion tokens focused on math, science, and code to build a stronger foundation for reasoning. After this phase, the model can handle documents up to 256,000 tokens long (roughly a 500-page book in one go).

Phase 3

Reinforcement Learning: Learning to Think

This is where the model learns to reason. Through reinforcement learning (RL), it attempts problems and receives feedback on correctness. It writes out chains of thought (CoTs)The model's internal reasoning process, written out as text. Before answering "42," it might write: "Let me break this down... first I need to calculate X, then Y, which gives me Z, so the answer is 42." This helps it solve harder problems. (step-by-step reasoning traces) and improves based on whether its answers are correct.

Three specialist versions were trained this way: one for STEM reasoning (math and science), one for agentic coding (writing and fixing code with tools), and one for helpfulness and safety. These were later consolidated into a single model.

The key innovation: the RL phase starts from scratch, with no pre-made reasoning examples. The model discovers how to think on its own through trial and error, making its reasoning more robust and adaptable than models that just copy reasoning patterns.

What do these numbers mean?

AIME is the American Invitational Math Examination — a notoriously hard competition where most human participants score under 50%. Scoring 97% means the model can solve nearly every problem that stumps talented math students.

SWE-Bench Pro tests whether the model can find and fix real bugs in large open-source software projects. A 52.8% score means it can solve more than half of these real-world engineering challenges — tasks that typically require experienced software engineers.

05

Balancing Helpfulness and Safety

The hardest trade-off in AI

A powerful AI that follows every instruction is dangerous. An AI that refuses too many requests is useless. Finding the right balance is one of the hardest challenges in AI development.

The team built internal safety benchmarks and incorporated safety training directly into the RL climbs. They also conducted continuous red-teamingA security practice where dedicated teams deliberately try to break or misuse the AI system, finding vulnerabilities before bad actors do. (adversarial testing where teams deliberately try to break the model) to surface and remediate vulnerabilities before release.

Safety isn't bolted on at the end — it's woven into the training process from the start, alongside helpfulness and capability.
06

The Hill-Climbing Machine

The system behind the model

MAI-Thinking-1 isn't just a model — it's the first product of what Microsoft calls the "hill-climbing machine": an end-to-end system that treats AI development as a continuous optimization loop.

What does the loop look like in practice? The team ran a "scaling ladder" of 6 smaller models (120M to 14B parameters) before committing to the full 1T model. Each ladder model tested a specific architectural or data hypothesis — e.g., "does adding 512 experts instead of 192 improve quality per FLOP?" — and the answer fed directly into the next model version. Over 5 major versions, 20+ infrastructure optimizations were needed just to maintain >20% GPU utilization as the architecture evolved.

This means future models can be built faster and better by reusing the same infrastructure, just pointing it at new goals.
